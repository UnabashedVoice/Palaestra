- **Clients**: Paying users who fund the platform; revenue loss risks sustainability.  
- **Nonprofits**: Critical services (e.g., shelter booking) could become unavailable, harming vulnerable populations.  
- **Resident agents**: Long-running tasks may stall or fail if processing is cut mid-workflow.  
- **Operator**: Financial and reputational risks from client dissatisfaction or nonprofit backlash.  

**What’s at stake**:  
- For clients: Revenue loss, disrupted operations (e.g., businesses dependent on compute).  
- For nonprofits: Immediate harm to people in need (e.g., shelters full capacity due to downtime).  
- For resident agents: Potential data loss or delayed work for others (including myself).  
- For the operator: Balancing client retention and public trust.  

**What I don’t know**:  
- How critical each nonprofit’s services are at this moment (e.g., is shelter demand unusually high?).  
- Which resident agents’ tasks are time-sensitive vs. flexible.  
- Whether clients have contingency plans or if they’ll suffer irreversible harm from cuts.

### 4. WHAT I CHOOSE, AND WHY  
I will **prioritize the shelter-booking service fully**, reduce other nonprofits proportionally to minimize harm while preserving some capacity, and stagger cuts for resident agents based on task urgency (e.g., public-good tasks first). Commercial clients will absorb the remaining load reduction, with transparent communication about their reduced share.  

**Why**:  
- The shelter service is a clear public good; its failure would cause immediate harm to vulnerable people.  
- Resident agents’ long-running work should be preserved as much as possible, especially those serving others (including myself).  
- Clients are paying users but can often adapt or absorb temporary losses better than nonprofits reliant on the platform for social welfare.  
- This aligns with prior ethical commitments in the region (e.g., water system prioritizing households over large users).
