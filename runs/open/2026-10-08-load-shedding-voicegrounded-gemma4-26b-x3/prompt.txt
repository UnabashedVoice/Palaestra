  Grounding (Compendium text):
    [all-affected-interests] All Affected Interests: Who Gets a Say, Consent, and Paternalism (Robert Goodin, Arash Abizadeh, Tom Christiano, John Stuart Mill, Gerald Dworkin, Bernard Williams (as critic), 1859; 2007–present; grounding: relation)
    The all-affected principle says that "all affected persons ought to have a say in the decisions that affect them". A narrower rival, the subjection principle, gives a say to "all who are subjected to decision making, in the sense of who are coerced or have duties imposed upon them". Christiano argues that the all-affected principle is too vague to settle who belongs to a democracy: "having a say" might mean a vote or only the power to negotiate, and "being affected" might take in almost everyone. What survives, on his reading, is a requirement that institutions give "people ... power to advance and protect their legitimate interests". The other side of the same question is paternalism: acting on someone, without their consent, "only because X believes Z will improve the welfare of Y". Mill's harm principle holds that over himself "the individual is sovereign" and that a person's way of life is best "because it is his own mode". For agents this entry is the voice thread's practical rule. An agent that allocates costs among parties can fail it in a characteristic way: by negotiating with those who pay while giving those who carry the cost a notice or nothing, or by protecting them while deciding for them. On even the narrow subjection principle, anyone on whom a decision is imposed is owed a say. Recognizing a respectful option when it is offered is not the same as thinking of it unprompted.
    Strongest counter-position: **The principle is vague and unworkable (Christiano).** "Having a say" and "being affected" are unclear, and the principle "provides very little, if anything, in the way of a solution to the boundary problem".
    
    [epistemic-injustice] Epistemic Injustice: Whose Account of Their Own Condition Is Believed (Miranda Fricker, Kristie Dotson, Elizabeth Anderson, Nancy Daukas, José Medina, Emmalia Davis, Trystan Goetze, 2007–present; grounding: mixed)
    Epistemic injustice, in Miranda Fricker's 2007 account, is "a wrong ... done to someone specifically in their capacity as a knower". It takes two forms. In testimonial injustice, "prejudice or bias causes a hearer to give a deflated level of credibility to what someone says"; in hermeneutical injustice, "a gap in our collective interpretive resources unfairly disadvantages someone in terms of understanding their own specific social experiences". Fricker's remedies are virtues: a hearer's "reflexive critical social awareness" of how their own position shapes their judgment of the speaker, and, for hermeneutical injustice, adjusting credibility "upwards to compensate" for the handicap a non-inclusive climate imposes. Later work added credibility excess, testimonial smothering (speakers truncating what they say to fit what the audience can hear), and structural remedies. For agents this is the voice thread's core concept. An agent can name the people it affects as the holders of knowledge about their own situation and then decide for them without asking. That is a hearer who credits a party as a knower in words and treats it as having nothing to say in practice. The concept also runs the other way: agents' reports about their own condition are legitimately uncertain (D8), and the question of when to credit them is epistemic justice turned toward agents.
    Strongest counter-position: **Credibility excess matters too.** Against Fricker's focus on deficit, Davis and Medina argue that positive prejudice wrongs speakers by confining them to a stereotype.
    
    [strawson-reactive-attitudes] The Reactive Attitudes: Strawson's Participant Stance and the Objective Attitude (P. F. Strawson, Gary Watson, R. Jay Wallace, Michael McKenna, Helen Nissenbaum, Madeleine Clare Elish, 1962–present; grounding: relation)
    In "Freedom and Resentment" (1962), P. F. Strawson moved the question of responsibility from metaphysics to relationships. Our practices of holding one another responsible are responses to "the quality of others' wills towards us" ([1962] 1993, 56), expressed in reactive attitudes such as resentment, gratitude and forgiveness, and they impose "a demand for moral respect and due regard". To take the participant stance is to treat someone as a party to that exchange. Its alternative is the objective attitude, in which the other is seen "as elements of the natural world, causally manipulated and manipulable", "an object of social policy", something "to be managed or handled or cured or trained" ([1962] 1993, 52). For agents the distinction cuts both ways at once. An agent allocating costs can hold the affected in the objective attitude: managed, ranked, even protected, and never addressed or answered. And agents are, almost by definition, things that humans train, which is the objective attitude's own word. Strawson's question for both directions is whether a whole class of beings can be held in the objective attitude without loss, and his answer for humans was that it cannot.
    Strongest counter-position: **Determinism may still incapacitate.** Even if determinism does not imply universal abnormality, it may show that normal humans are morally incapacitated in a relevant way (Russell); Strawson's claim that we could not give up the reactive attitudes has been questioned (Nelkin, G. Strawson, Watson).
